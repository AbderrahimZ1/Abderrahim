Linking Accident Data and Physics-Based Modeling for Hydrogen Fire Safety

First author: A. Zermane · Fire Technology 62(5), Article 128.

954 hydrogen incidentsPhysics-based reconstructionMonte Carlo uncertainty
Study figure

What the framework does

Four HIAD 2.1 tables were consolidated into a master incident dataset. First-order engineering relationships were then used to estimate fuel inventory, heat-release rate, flame length, explosion-energy potential and notional blast indicators.

Why this matters: hydrogen incident databases often report event descriptions and consequences without the physical quantities needed for engineering comparison.

Open the DOI →

Why reconstruction was necessary

Hydrogen incident records frequently contain qualitative descriptions, consequence categories and incomplete inventories. The paper therefore reconstructs screening-level descriptors while recording the assumptions used to fill missing fields.

Evidence and method

The workflow consolidated 954 hydrogen-related incidents. First-order relationships were used to estimate fuel mass, heat-release rate, flame-length scale, explosion-energy potential and notional blast indicators. Monte Carlo analysis propagated uncertainty from incomplete records into the reconstructed outputs.

Main finding

The reconstructed incidents span several orders of magnitude in potential energy release, yet simple relationships with fatalities and damage remain weak. Exposure, confinement, ignition timing, mitigation and missing reporting fields materially affect the observed consequences.

What the paper does not claim

The estimated quantities are not measurements and do not validate a detailed consequence model. Their value is comparative: they create a transparent bridge between incident learning and physics-based interpretation and reveal which fields future hydrogen incident databases should record.

Research significance

This paper is the third flagship publication and the first dedicated fire-safety paper in the first-author programme. It extends earlier evidence-led risk work into hydrogen fire and explosion science while retaining the same emphasis on traceability and uncertainty.
